Lightweight division without an undisputed leader

Abdullah Mason is one of the most talented lightweight athletes right now. However, the division itself is in a period of rebuilding. Stars like Shakur Stevenson, Devin Haney, and Teofimo Lopez have moved to heavier divisions. Gervonta Davis still has not returned from yet another long hiatus due to legal issues. Mason is the potential future of the division for the next few years — provided he proves himself fully.

His fight against Sam Noakes proved the kid has the skills. He looked great early and came close to a stoppage win. But he still lacks composure and the experience of truly big nights. In that fight, Mason won convincingly, but almost let the lead slip when Noakes started to come back. Bell is the next challenge of the same scale.

Mason: numbers and style

At 22, Mason carries a 20-0 record with 17 stoppages — an 85% finishing rate. For context, he has logged just 71 rounds as a professional. The average Mason fight doesn't go beyond three or four rounds. He presses, dominates through speed and long arms, and finishes. He doesn't wait for the opponent to break — he picks him apart.

A reach of 188 cm on a 174 cm frame is an unusual ratio. It gives him a range advantage that fighters of his height rarely enjoy — and he uses it.

Bell: experience vs. talent

Albert Bell is a classically solid American boxer — undefeated, but without marquee names on his record. At 33, this is his first genuinely major challenge: a title fight against a young talent being compared to Terence Crawford and projected for a big future. A chance to become champion overnight and make a real statement.

His record reads 28-0, but only nine of those wins came inside the distance — a 32% stoppage rate. Bell mostly wins by decision, manages rounds, and accumulates points. He is not a puncher. That said, three of his last five wins came by stoppage, which may suggest something has shifted in his approach or preparation.

Bell has 165 professional rounds against Mason's 71 — nearly 100 more. That is his central argument: experience, patience, and the ability to read a fight in the later rounds after six or seven frames together.

How the fight plays out

This promises an interesting tactical contest. Mason is an aggressor who presses and hunts the finish. Bell is larger, more experienced, and has more professional rounds in the bank. But round count decides nothing on its own — it is an additional factor, not the defining one. What matters more is what each man can show when they stand in front of each other, and whether the more experienced fighter can keep Mason from running wild.

Mason is dangerous from the opening bell. The challenger needs a specific plan for those early moments. Will Bell have enough experience and size to weather the start and drag the fight into deep water? If so, Mason faces another difficult test. It will be interesting to see how he handles it.
